1. Flatness

From inside the cave...

Inside the cave, the people sit facing a blank stone wall where dark shapes flicker across its surface. They have never seen the object that creates these forms; all they know is the shifting silhouettes seen before them. At first, the shadow appears as a square, balanced and complete. It seems solid and dependable. But when the shadow suddenly stretches upward and becomes taller before their eyes, the people interpret this as growth. To them, something in their world is expanding, becoming more powerful or significant. They do not suspect manipulation or perspective; they believe they are witnessing transformation itself. Later, the shape shifts again and compresses into a thin vertical line that nearly disappears. Panic spreads among them. The one solid presence now appears as weak and fragile, as if it is collapsing or fading from existence. The shadow is their only visible reference point; they treat its changes as evidence that reality itself is unstable. They can not imagine that something outside their view is being scaled or moved. The shadow is not a copy to them; it is the thing itself. Every alteration feels meaningful, and almost alive, as though the world beyond the wall is unpredictably changing.

Nick Sousani's concept of Flatness, to my understanding, refers to a restricted way of seeing that fixes complexity into one single way of interpretation. Flatness occurs when we accept surface appearances as complete truths, without looking at the larger systems or dimensions that produce them. It is a way of thinking that eliminates depth, visually and conceptually. The people inside the cave exist entirely within this flattened perspective. Their world is two-dimensional, literally, a shadow cast on stone. They have no access to the object creating the projection, and they can not imagine depth beyond the surface. Every meaning they come up with is based on outlines and movement across a flat wall. When the shadow stretches, they interpret growth; when it narrows, they interpret disappearance. They never question whether these changes might result from something that is beyond their line of sight. Their understanding is confined to what appears immediately before them. In this way, their worldview is flat; it is reduced, compressed, and limited to a single visible layer of reality. They mistake representation for truth and projection for presence, building an entire belief system from incomplete information.

2. Seeing Double

From outside the cave...

Outside the cave, the illusion is easy to understand. A solid three-dimensional cube stands in open space between a bright directional light and the interior wall of the cave. The light shines steadily from one side, and wherever the cube blocks that light, a shadow is cast onto the wall inside. From this position, the relationship is clear: the shadow is not an independent object, but the result of light being interrupted by form. When the cube is moved slightly forward and reduced in size, it occupies less visual space in front of the light, and its shadow on the wall becomes smaller and shifts position. Nothing inside the cave is changing on its own; the movement of the shadow is directly caused by the cube’s new placement and scale in three-dimensional space. Later, when the cube is pulled farther back and enlarged in scale, it blocks more of the light, and the shadow grows larger and stretches across the wall. From the outside, these changes are simple adjustments of geometry and distance. The cube remains the same object throughout; only its spatial relationship to the light shifts. What appears dramatic and mysterious inside the cave is, from this perspective, a predictable interaction between light, object, and surface.

Nick Sousanis’s concept of Seeing Double means learning to have two perspectives at once. It is the ability to recognize both the surface image and the deeper meaning that produces it. Seeing Double is not about rejecting appearances, but about understanding that appearances are part of a larger system. In the case of the cave, the people outside can see both the cube and the shadow simultaneously. They understand that when the cube moves forward and shrinks, the shadow inside becomes smaller because less space is being blocked from the light. When the cube moves backward and grows larger, the shadow expands because more light is being interrupted. The same physical action produces two different experiences. Inside the cave, the people see a shadow that appears to grow or disappear and believe reality itself is changing. Outside the cave, observers see a cube adjusting its position in space and recognize the shadow as a projection. Seeing Double connects these viewpoints. It reveals that the shadow and the cube are not two separate realities, but linked expressions of the same event. True understanding emerges when we recognize both dimensions at once, the visible effect on the wall and the invisible spatial cause behind it. By stepping outside the cave, perception gains depth, and reality expands beyond the flat surface of the shadow.
